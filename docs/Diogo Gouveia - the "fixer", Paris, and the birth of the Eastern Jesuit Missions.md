Francis Xavier died in the island of Shangshuan in December of 1552, as he waited an opportunity to enter China. 

He would later become a Saint and represents to this day the archetype of the Jesuit missionary in the East. One of the founders of the Society and the organizer of the Jesuit first and most spectacular overseas operation. He alone made the Society transition from a group of devoted young men to a global organization which shaped the future of the world in many aspects.

But how this came to be? How was it possible? To ask a simpler question: how far back in the history of Francis and his mates must we go to find a plausible seed moment from which this enormous tree grew.

We need to go back to Paris, to the Quartier Latin, in the years 1520. More specifically to the Collége de Sainte Barbare, where young Francis met for the first time those who will shape his future. The people he met, the place where they met and the web of connections around that place, constitute the basic plumbing that will conduct the energy of the vast systemic changes that were taking place at the time. 

This is a story with many angles and many points of view.  Here we will focus first on the point of view of Diogo de Gouveia, the Elder, head of the college, former rector of the Sorbonne and the french fixer of Portuguese king John the III. In Gouveia's college, Francis, Inácio de Loyola, Simão Rodrigues and Pedro Fabro met. They would become the core of the future society. 

Diogo came to Paris as a student, probably in 1492, with a grant from the Portuguese king John the II and later from king Manuel II. From 1513 onwards, in his early 40's he is on record writing to the portuguese king about deals related to french corsairs, a complex system of attacks, ransoms, and compensations. For years Gouveia will be the fixer of the multiple incidents that french corsairs created in the newly sea routes to Brasil and the East operated by Portuguese ships.  
In 1520 he takes charge of the College of Santa Barbara. Santa Bárbara was one of the colleges surrounding the University of Paris. Created in 1460 as a "private" college by a professor of the university it had changed hands in 1512, bought by the owner of another college. In 1520 Gouveia rents the college.

Colleges were a good business in Paris, like in other university cities of the time.The model is simple to understand. In order to enter university students had to learn latin and get the basic preparatory education. Also, during their life as university students, they needed a place to stay and study.  So colleges provided the living and learning environment, together with discipline and preparatory courses. These courses could be rather sofisticated and a college would compete with others with the quality of the resident professors.

Gouveia had a pretty competitive operation. But, more importantly, he had a steady source of income: the portuguese students sent to Paris
